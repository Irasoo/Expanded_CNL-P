==================== USER INPUT 1/1 ====================
Brand:
SunTrace

Product:
A facial sunscreen that is transparent when applied normally. The package includes reusable UV-sensitive stickers. When a sticker is placed on the skin before sun exposure and removed later, the protected area remains unchanged while the surrounding exposed area temporarily reveals the sticker shape.

Target audience:
Young adults aged 18-30 who understand that sunscreen is important but frequently forget to reapply it during outdoor activities.

Objective:
Make the invisible protective effect of sunscreen visually understandable and motivate users to remember sunscreen application and reapplication.

Key product benefit:
The sunscreen provides SPF 50 protection, while the included UV-sensitive demonstration stickers make the effect of sun exposure easier to visualize.

Channels:
TikTok, Instagram Reels, and university social media communities.

Budget:
Low to medium.

Constraints:
Use only the real sunscreen and included demonstration stickers. Do not claim that the temporary visual demonstration measures exact UV damage. Do not use celebrities or expensive locations. The idea must be understandable within a short video.

==================== AGENT OUTPUT ====================
FINAL CREATIVE CONCEPT
======================
**“Your Face Keeps a SunTrace”**

SunTrace turns its reusable UV-sensitive sticker into an **outdoor receipt with a check-out time**: a visible marker that says, “You have been out long enough to check in and reapply.” The sticker reveal keeps sunscreen’s invisible benefit central, while a recurring visual check-in makes the reapplication habit feel relevant during the actual moments young adults lose track of time outdoors.

Each TikTok, Reel, or university social post opens on a familiar outdoor transition, not a generic sunscreen routine: leaving a lecture for an outdoor lunch, arriving at a park, starting a bike ride, joining friends on a patio, or settling in for a campus event. A quick close-up shows SunTrace SPF 50 being applied normally and one included sticker placed on the skin. The creator taps a simple on-screen SunTrace check-in marker before heading out.

Fast cuts show the activity naturally stretching longer than expected. At the transition point, the same check-in marker returns visually, paired with the product in hand: **“Still outside? SunTrace check-in.”** The creator reapplies SunTrace before continuing their day.

The final beat returns to the sticker. In a clear close-up, the creator removes it to reveal their outdoor receipt: the area beneath the sticker remains unchanged while the surrounding exposed skin may temporarily show the sticker shape. The result is framed as proof that the day left a visible trace, not as a damage score. A concise line appears directly with the reveal: **“A temporary sun-exposure demonstration, not a measure of UV damage.”**

The closing mnemonic is consistent across every episode: **“Out longer than planned? Check in. Reapply SunTrace.”** SunTrace SPF 50 is shown at both application moments, making the product the practical action behind the visual payoff.

The series remains low-to-medium budget and repeatable: real people, everyday outdoor settings, SunTrace sunscreen, and its included reusable stickers. To protect the reveal, shoots are captured after meaningful time outdoors in conditions where contrast is visible. When the result is subtle, the edit still lands on the behavior rather than exaggerating the outcome: the creator removes the sticker, acknowledges the lighter trace, and immediately performs the recognizable SunTrace check-in and reapplication.

Evaluation reasons:
The concept gives the included UV-sensitive sticker a distinctive recurring role as an outdoor receipt and connects its visual reveal to the real behavioral problem of forgetting to reapply. This makes the invisible benefit of SPF 50 easier to understand and keeps SunTrace central at both the initial application and the reapplication moment. The check-in mnemonic is clear, repeatable, and relevant to young adults whose outdoor plans extend unexpectedly. The sticker reveal also offers a strong short-video hook, although the receipt and check-out language is a metaphor rather than an immediately obvious product truth, so the idea is differentiated but not exceptionally original. Execution is practical for TikTok, Reels, and university social channels using everyday settings and real product materials, but it depends on sufficient exposure time, suitable conditions, and a visible contrast in the sticker reveal. Subtle or inconsistent results could weaken the payoff, and the disclaimer may compete for attention in a very short edit.

Refinement suggestions:
Make the reapplication behavior the clearest first takeaway by showing a simple, recognizable time or activity cue alongside the check-in marker, while keeping the receipt metaphor as a supporting device rather than the main explanation. Establish the sticker demonstration quickly and explain in plain language that it visualizes exposure without measuring exact UV damage; use concise on-screen wording or a caption so the disclaimer does not interrupt the reveal. Build several repeatable video templates around common moments when plans run long, such as lunch becoming an afternoon or a short bike ride becoming a full day outdoors. Plan for subtle results by defining a consistent close-up, lighting approach, and edit structure that still communicates the check-in and reapplication action when the sticker shape is faint. Avoid implying that the sticker determines when reapplication is medically required; frame it as a memorable reminder to follow sunscreen directions and reapply during extended outdoor activity.
