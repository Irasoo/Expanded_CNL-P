==================== USER INPUT 1/1 ====================
Brand:
SunTrace

Product:
A facial sunscreen that is transparent when applied normally. The package includes reusable UV-sensitive stickers. When a sticker is placed on the skin before sun exposure and removed later, the protected area remains unchanged while the surrounding exposed area temporarily reveals the sticker shape.

Target audience:
Young adults aged 18-30 who understand that sunscreen is important but frequently forget to reapply it during outdoor activities.

Objective:
Make the invisible protective effect of sunscreen visually understandable and motivate users to remember sunscreen application and reapplication.

Key product benefit:
The sunscreen provides SPF 50 protection, while the included UV-sensitive demonstration stickers make the effect of sun exposure easier to visualize.

Channels:
TikTok, Instagram Reels, and university social media communities.

Budget:
Low to medium.

Constraints:
Use only the real sunscreen and included demonstration stickers. Do not claim that the temporary visual demonstration measures exact UV damage. Do not use celebrities or expensive locations. The idea must be understandable within a short video.
================== END USER INPUT 1/1 ==================

==================== AGENT OUTPUT ====================
FINAL CREATIVE CONCEPT
**SunTrace: “Ten Minutes, Edited.”**

SunTrace turns the familiar “we’ll only be outside for ten minutes” promise into a visual running joke and a practical sunscreen cue. Every short video begins with a student applying transparent SunTrace SPF 50, placing an included UV-sensitive sticker on their cheek or forearm, and typing **“10 MINUTES”** on screen as they head outside.

As the plan expands, the original caption stays fixed while reality edits it. Coffee becomes lunch, a walk across campus becomes an afternoon on the lawn, or one quick game becomes several. With each change, **“10 MINUTES”** is visibly crossed out and replaced: **“30 MINUTES”**, **“ONE MORE HOUR”**, then **“OK, PLANS CHANGED.”** Fast cuts, changing timestamps, and friends entering the frame make the escalation immediate, relatable, and varied from video to video.

At the turning point, the student removes the sticker. The protected area remains unchanged while the surrounding exposed area temporarily reveals the sticker shape, making otherwise invisible sun exposure visible. The reveal lands with the recurring line: **“Plans changed. Check your SPF.”** Beside it, concise on-screen text reads: **“A visual reminder, not a measure of UV damage or a reapplication timer.”**

The student checks the SunTrace label and reapplies according to the product directions before continuing outdoors. Videos can also feature relevant moments such as sweating, swimming, or towel drying, always directing viewers back to the package instructions rather than implying that the sticker determines when to reapply.

The format is built for TikTok, Instagram Reels, and university social communities: students recreate the escalating-plan edit in everyday campus locations using only SunTrace sunscreen and its included stickers. Different plans, participants, and crossed-out time captions keep the series fresh, while the consistent **“10 MINUTES”** opening, sticker reveal, and **“Plans changed. Check your SPF.”** payoff make it instantly recognizable.

SunTrace owns every key beat: its transparent SPF 50 protection is applied at the start, its sticker creates the memorable visual demonstration, and its label guides the real reapplication decision. The concept turns a common young-adult behavior, underestimating time outdoors, into a simple habit: when the plan gets edited, check your SPF.

Evaluation reasons: The concept has a strong product-specific visual device: the included UV-sensitive sticker turns transparent SPF 50 protection into a visible reveal that can work well in short-form video. The escalating '10 MINUTES' premise is familiar and relatable for young adults whose outdoor plans often expand, while the repeated crossed-out captions and 'Plans changed. Check your SPF.' line create a recognizable series format. Strategic fit is especially strong because the concept connects a real audience behavior, forgetting to reapply during changing plans, to both the demonstration sticker and the product label instructions. It also respects the constraints by avoiding unsupported claims, celebrities, expensive locations, and the suggestion that the sticker measures UV damage or determines reapplication timing. Audience impact should be high because the reveal is immediately understandable and the social behavior is easy to recognize, though the core 'plans expand' joke may become predictable across multiple executions. Execution is practical for TikTok, Reels, and university communities using everyday locations, students, the real product, and the included stickers. The main practical risks are that the visual reveal depends on suitable exposure and timing, and each video must explain the demonstration limitation without weakening the payoff.
Refinement suggestions: Preserve the escalating-plan structure, sticker reveal, and recurring line, but vary the behavioral trigger beyond elapsed time so the series does not become repetitive: include sweating, swimming, towel drying, or spontaneous changes in plans while keeping the label as the authority for reapplication. Make the opening and reveal visually consistent enough to build brand recognition, but use a tighter recurring edit such as the same on-screen plan counter or notification style. Keep the disclaimer concise and visually subordinate to the reveal, while clearly stating that the sticker is a visual demonstration rather than a UV-damage measurement or reapplication timer. Ensure the product application and sticker placement are shown in a way that accurately reflects package directions, and avoid implying that the unchanged area proves a precise level of protection. A final shot of the product label and a simple check-and-reapply action would strengthen the practical behavior change without adding production complexity.
